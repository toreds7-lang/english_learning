Part 1 · RhythmRule 1 of 22Korean trap ★★★

English is stress-timed: long beats and short beats

영어는 강세 박자 언어: 긴 박과 짧은 박이 번갈아 나온다

Stressed syllables: longer, louder, higher, with an up-down pitch shape. Unstressed: flatter, quicker, lower. The contrast carries the meaning.
  1. The ear goes to stressed syllables. Rachel: when every syllable has the same length, “there's no way for the ear to know which words are the most important.” 모든 음절을 같은 길이로 말하면, 듣는 사람이 핵심 단어를 찾기 어렵다
  2. Content words carry the beats (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs). Function words shrink so the beats can pop out.
  3. Shrinking is correct, not lazy. “to the” becomes tə thə and “because of” becomes kəz əv, so STORE and JOB stand out.

Korean habit

Korean is syllable-timed: each syllable gets about the same time. Spoken that way, English sounds even and makes the listener work harder. Fix: exaggerate. Make stressed syllables twice as long, and make the rest as short as you can.

한국어는 음절마다 길이가 비슷하다 → 영어에서는 강세 음절을 과장해서 길게, 나머지는 최대한 짧게

Part 1 · RhythmRule 2 of 22Korean trap ★★★

Every word has one peak

단어마다 가장 강한 음절은 하나뿐이다

A 3-syllable word has one of three shapes: DA-da-da (popular) · da-DA-da (decided) · da-da-DA (every day). Learn the shape together with the word.
  1. The peak is higher and longer and has the up-down shape: a-BOUT, em-BAR-rass-ment (with a smaller secondary stress on “em”).
  2. Shorten the short ones. Rachel: Spanish speakers need longer long syllables; “pretty much everyone else needs to make their short syllables shorter”, even a bit muddled. 약한 음절을 더 짧고 흐릿하게 — 대부분의 학습자에게 필요한 연습
  3. Practise the rhythm first, then the word: “da-DA-da… decided”. The dictionary mark ˈ sits before the stressed syllable: senˈsation.

Korean habit

Loanwords such as 바나나, 호텔, 초콜릿, 컴퓨터 give every syllable the same weight and often add syllables. American “chocolate” has two syllables, CHOC-lit, not 초-콜-릿.

Part 1 · RhythmRule 3 of 22Korean trap ★★★

Schwa: the sound of unstressed English

슈와 /ə/: 강세 없는 음절과 기능어는 힘을 빼고 짧게

Unstressed vowels become ə (schwa): tiny, relaxed, low in pitch. Little function words take it in sentences.
for → fər
to → tə / də
of → əv / ə
and → ən / n
can → kən
them → əm
at → ət
was → wəz
your → yər
that → ðət
a / the → ə / ðə
her → ər
  1. Schwa is always unstressed. The jaw barely drops; lips, cheeks and tongue stay relaxed: sofa, about, ability.
  2. Before L, M, N or R the schwa disappears into the consonant: father = fath-r, button = butt-n.
  3. Function words (prepositions, articles, pronouns, helping verbs) are weak inside a sentence. Content words keep their full vowels. 기능어(전치사·관사·대명사·조동사)는 문장 속에서 약하게

Korean habit

Reading little words with their dictionary vowel: for → 포, to → 투, and → 앤드, of → 오브. They then weigh as much as the content words and the rhythm goes flat.

Part 1 · RhythmRule 4 of 22Korean trap ★★

Can vs. can't: listen for the vowel and the cut

can은 약하게 /kən/, can't는 강하게 + 끊기는 T

can = kən: weak, smooth, unstressed. can't = KÆN’: full vowel, stressed, cut off by a stop T.
  1. “Can” is a helping verb, so in a sentence it is unstressed and takes schwa: “I kən DO it.” “You kən come” — Rachel: “it's almost not even there.”
  2. “Can't” never reduces. It keeps æ and the stress, and its T is usually a stop (no puff): “I CAN'T do it.” In the long version Rachel checked 20 real examples; only 2 had a true T.
  3. At the end of a sentence both keep æ: “I can.” is a little longer and smoother; “I can't.” is shorter and more abrupt. 문장 끝: can은 조금 길고 부드럽게, can't는 짧고 뚝 끊기게

Korean habit

A full, stressed 캔 for “can” makes listeners hear “can't”. A released 캔트 adds a syllable. Fix: weak kən for can; stressed KAN plus a silent stop for can't.

Contractions and gonna, wanna, gotta

축약형이 말하기의 기본값: I'm, it'll, could've, gonna

In speech, contractions are the normal form. Each one has a fixed sound. Learn them as units.
WrittenSayNote
could've · should'vekʊdəv · ʃʊdəvextra weak syllable (“could of”)
I'll · you'll · we'llaɪəl · yuəl · wɪlends in a dark L
it'll · it'dɪɾəl · ɪɾədT flaps, adds a syllable
you're · they'reyər · ðɛryou're = “yer”
going to + verbgʌnə“gonna” only before a verb

  1. 've after a consonant adds a weak syllable: could've sounds like “could of”, which is why some natives misspell it that way.
  2. 'll ends in a dark L (the back of the tongue): I'll, they'll. We'll sounds just like “will”.
  3. gonna replaces “going to” before a verb: “I'm gonna put it on high.” For movement keep “going to”: “I'm going to the store.” gonna는 “going to + 동사”일 때만. 장소로 갈 때는 going to

Korean habit

Full forms everywhere (I will, I am going to) sound stiff in conversation, and “I'll” said as 아일 without a dark L sounds like “I”.

Part 1 · MelodyRule 6 of 22Korean trap ★★

Intonation: which way does the pitch go?

억양: 평서문 ↘ · 예/아니오 질문 ↗ · 의문사 질문 ↘

Statements ↘ · Yes/no questions ↗ (did, does, is, can…) · WH-questions ↘ (how, why, where, who, which) · Not finished yet → small ↗
  1. Statements start higher and end lowest, even long ones with ups and downs in the middle.
  2. Pitch shows attitude. “Are you serious? ↗” sounds open and concerned; “Are you serious? ↘” sounds like a judgment. Same words. 같은 문장도 억양에 따라 걱정(↗) 또는 비난(↘)
  3. A small rise in the middle means “there's more”: “I made some really good friends here ↗ … and …”. A list rises on each item, then falls at the end.

Korean habit

Raising the pitch at the end of every question makes WH-questions sound unsure. And a flat melody sounds foreign even when every sound is right. Rachel: speaking “in the intonation of another language… will still sound very foreign.”

Part 2 · LinkingRule 7 of 22Korean trap ★★

Move the last consonant onto the next vowel

자음으로 끝나고 모음으로 시작하면: 자음을 다음 단어로 넘긴다 (연음)

Word ending in a consonant + word starting with a vowel → say them as one: ten hours a day → te-nour-za-day
  1. Think of the consonant as starting the next word: “it's his anniversary” → it-si-za-nni-ver-sa-ry, “this is” → thi-sis, “I'm on” → I-mon.
  2. If that puts a T between vowels, it flaps: “what about” → wha-də-bout, “forget about it” → for-ge-də-bou-dit.
  3. Weak H words drop the H and link: he, him, her, his, have, has, had. “tell her” → tel-ler, “ask her” → as-ker, “what have you done” → wha-dəv-you done, “get him” = “get 'em”. 약하게 말하는 he/him/her/his/have는 h를 빼고 앞 단어에 붙인다
  4. No linking across punctuation: “At first he never came; he now comes regularly” keeps the second H.

Korean head start

You already do this inside Korean words: 음악 → [으막], 먹어 → [머거]. Do the same across English word boundaries. The habit to drop is a tiny pause or catch in the throat before every word that starts with a vowel.

Part 2 · LinkingRule 8 of 22Korean trap ★★★

Consonant meets consonant: hold it, don't add “으”

자음 + 자음: 사이에 “으” 모음을 넣지 않는다

No vowel between two consonants. Same consonant → say it once. Stop consonant (p b t d k g) + consonant → stop the air, don't release.
  1. Same consonant, one sound (a little longer): gas station, some might, big guy, bad dog.
  2. Stops before a consonant are held, not popped: hot today, peanut butter, flip phone, nap time, what's up. Rachel: not “ha today” with no stop, and not a full released T either.
  3. Never add a vowel in between. Rachel names this mistake: “hot sauce” becomes “hot-uh sauce” when students add a schwa to link. 연결하려고 자음 사이에 “어/으”를 넣는 실수
  4. For new pairs, practise the two sounds separately, then slide slowly from one to the other: tough one = f → w.

Korean head start

받침 already works this way: 밥, 책, 곧 end without a release. Use that in “hot dog” [핟 독].

Korean habit

The loanword habit of adding 으: desk → 데스크, milk → 밀크, bus stop → 버스 스톱, text → 텍스트. Each 으 adds a syllable English doesn't have.

Part 3 · T & DRule 9 of 22Korean trap ★★

One letter, four T's: check in this order

T는 네 가지 소리: 진짜 T · 플랩 T · 멈춤 T · 생략 T

  1. Starts the word or a stressed syllable; or in st, str, tr; or ends in ct, ft, lt, st, pt (not before another consonant)tap · at-TEND · stop · soft · sleptTRUE T
  2. T + ən, or the end of a word or syllable before a consonant or a pause; also final -ntbutton · mountain · wait · about · fitness · sentSTOP T
  3. After N in an unstressed syllable, or squeezed between two consonantstwenty · interview · wanted · last night · just wantedDROPPED
  4. Between two vowel sounds, also across words; or after R before a vowelwater · auto · what about · get it · partyFLAP ≈ D
  1. Rule 1 beats rule 4. auto (flap) vs a-TONE (true T): the T starts the stressed syllable, so it stays true.
  2. Rules are about sounds, not letters. In “night working” the T follows a diphthong sound, so it is a stop, not dropped.

Korean habit

Releasing every T with a puff and a vowel: 트. “I just wanted to say that” has four written T's; in Rachel's Friends clip only one is a true T.

Part 3 · T & DRule 10 of 22Korean head start ★

Flap T: a quick tap, like Korean ㄹ

모음 사이의 T/D = 혀끝으로 한 번 톡: 한국어 모음 사이 ㄹ과 거의 같다

T or D between vowel sounds (or after R, before a vowel) → one light tap of the tongue, no stop of air: water → wa-der, matter = madder.
  1. No stop. A D at the start of “dad” stops the air; the D in “daddy” doesn't. The flap T is that second, smooth kind of D. “We love to smooth out the line.”
  2. It works across words: get it → ge-dit, a lot of → ə-lo-dəv, what about → wha-də-bout, put it on.
  3. Not when the T starts a stressed syllable (a-TEND, a-TONE) and not before ən (button is a stop T, next rule).

Korean head start

Rachel names Korean among the languages that have this sound, usually written as the letter R. It is the ㄹ between vowels in 바라, 다리, 나라. Try water ≈ 와러, better ≈ 베러, get it ≈ 게릿. Keep it one light tap, and keep the American R (rule 14) different: “it is not at all related to the R sound in American English.”

Part 3 · T & DRule 11 of 22Korean trap ★★

Stop T: close the air, no puff

멈춤 T: 공기를 막기만 하고 터뜨리지 않는다 (받침 ㄷ처럼)

At the end of a word (wait, that's, get dinner) and in T + ən (button, mountain): stop the airflow, don't release a T.
  1. Stop the air in your throat; the tongue may lift to the T position, but no puff comes out: wait, not now, get dinner.
  2. T + ən: stop, then let the N out through the nose with no vowel in between. The tongue doesn't move, because T and N share a position: mountain, button, written, cotton, curtain, sentence, Manhattan. button = “벋-n”: 혀는 그대로, 공기만 끊었다가 코로 n
  3. A stop T is not “no T”. “wait” vs “way”: wait is shorter and cut off; way is longer and its pitch falls away.

Korean head start

A stop T is a 받침 ㄷ/ㅅ: 곧, 낫. Closed, no 트.

Korean habit

버튼, 마운틴, 센텐스: a released T plus a full vowel. American “button” has no vowel after the T.

Part 3 · T & DRule 12 of 22Korean trap ★★

After N, the T or D often disappears

N 뒤의 T·D는 자주 사라진다: twenty → 트웨니, friendship → 프렌십

NT + unstressed vowel → drop the T: twenty → twen-ny. ND + consonant → drop the D: friendship → frien-ship.
  1. T after N, before an unstressed vowel: “nine times out of ten” Americans drop it in identify and twenty. Also interview, internet, center, wanted, counting, disappointed.
  2. Across words too: “want another” sounds the same as “one another”; “want to” → wanna.
  3. Keep the T when stress follows the N or the N and T sit in different syllables: in-to, en-try, an-TIQUE.
  4. D after N before a consonant drops: friendship, grandkids, kindness, errands, around the, found my, second time. Before a vowel it stays: “My friend is here.” N + D + 자음이면 D 생략, N + D + 모음이면 D 유지

Korean habit

Reading the spelling: 트웬티, 인터넷, 인터뷰, 원티드. Fix: let the N flow straight into the vowel. This is normal American speech, not only slang: Rachel hears it even in speeches.

Part 3 · EndingsRule 13 of 22Korean trap ★★★

-ed and -s copy the last sound

-ed와 -s는 앞 소리를 따라간다: 무성음 → t/s, 유성음 → d/z

Base word ends…-ed-s
After a voiceless soundt packed, laughed, washed, watcheds cats, ships, books
After a voiced sound or a voweld saved, turned, carriedz trees, dogs, apples
Extra syllableɪd after t, d: wanted, neededɪz after s z sh zh ch j: wishes, boxes, ages
  1. Only ɪd and ɪz add a syllable. watched, helped and changed are one syllable each; wishes and ages are two.
  2. Vowels are longer before a voiced ending: badge vs batch, bed vs bet. The ending of “batch” is also stronger.
  3. Voiced endings are weak at the end of a phrase (flowers ≈ a soft s), and get stronger when linked to a vowel: “flowers on the table”. 문장 끝의 유성 자음은 약하게, 모음과 연결되면 또렷하게

Korean habit

Adding 으: watched → 워치드 (3 syllables instead of 1), helped → 헬프드, books → 북스. English: watched = wɑtʃt, one syllable.

Part 4 · ConsonantsRule 14 of 22Korean trap ★★★

R and L are two different tongues

R은 혀끝이 어디에도 닿지 않고, L은 혀끝이 윗잇몸에 닿는다. 한국어 ㄹ은 둘 다 아니다

R: tongue tip pulled back, touching nothing, lips rounded. You can hold it: rrrrr. L: tongue tip touches just behind the top teeth.
  1. R: the tip pulls back and up; the sides of the tongue touch the upper side teeth; lips push forward (a tight circle at the start of a word: race, read); very little jaw drop. Hold it 5–10 seconds while you practise.
  2. Light L (before a vowel): tip on the ridge behind the top teeth, or between the teeth; lips relaxed: light, play, glass, blanch.
  3. Dark L (after a vowel): the back of the tongue pulls down and back, with an “uh”-like sound before the L, and the tip may not lift at all: feel = fee-uhl, pool, call, candle, girl. 끝소리 L은 혀 뒤쪽으로: feel ≈ 피-얼, call ≈ 커-얼
  4. A tap is not an R. Rachel: if you can't hold it out, it isn't the American R. Dropping the jaw a lot is another sign of a hollow R.

Korean habit

One ㄹ for both, so right = light, rice = lice, pray = play. The dark L is dropped or turned into 을: feel → 필, call → 콜.

Part 4 · ConsonantsRule 15 of 22Korean trap ★★★

TH: the tip of the tongue between the teeth

TH: 혀끝만 살짝 이 사이로, 공기는 계속 흐르게 (ㅆ·ㄷ 아님)

Just the tip of a relaxed tongue between the teeth; air flows with no stop. θ = air only (think). ð = with voice (this).
  1. Only the very tip comes out. The teeth surround it without clamping; the tongue stays soft so it can vibrate. Too far forward makes the next sound hard.
  2. It is continuous. “There shouldn't be any pressure in the mouth or a stop quality.” A stop makes it T/D (ㄷ, ㄸ); a hiss with the tongue behind the teeth makes it S (ㅆ).
  3. Shortcut in quick little words (the, this, that, them, these): the tip can press behind the teeth instead of coming through. 빠른 기능어 the/this/that은 혀끝을 이 뒤에 대도 된다
  4. θ think, three, birthday, worth, nothing · ð this, other, brother, either, though

Korean habit

think → 씽크 (= sink), three → 쓰리 (≈ tree), this → 디스 (= dis), mouth → 마우스 (= mouse). Mirror check: you should see the tip of your tongue on every unvoiced TH.

Part 4 · ConsonantsRule 16 of 22Korean trap ★★★

F and V use lip + teeth; P and B use both lips

F·V: 아랫입술 안쪽을 윗니에 대고 바람 · P·B: 두 입술을 붙였다 터뜨림

F/V: the inside of the bottom lip lightly touches the top front teeth and air keeps flowing (V adds voice). P/B: both lips close, then pop open.
  1. F/V: the bottom lip lifts to the bottom edge of the top front teeth; the top lip lifts a little to make room. It's the inside of the lip, so don't curl it in.
  2. P/B are stops: lips press together, air builds, then releases. At the end of a phrase or before a consonant the release can be skipped: what's up, nap time.
  3. Mirror check: for F/V you see your top teeth; for P/B you see no teeth. 거울로 확인: F·V는 윗니가 보이고, P·B는 이가 안 보인다

Korean habit

Korean has no F or V: coffee → 커피 (≈ copy), fan → 팬 (= pan), very → 베리 (= berry), vote → 보트 (= boat).

Part 4 · ConsonantsRule 17 of 22Korean trap ★★★

S, Z and SH: lips and buzz tell them apart

S는 입술 편하게, SH는 입술을 앞으로 · Z는 S에 성대 울림 (ㅈ 아님)

S: lips relaxed, tongue tip low. SH: lips push forward, tongue arched up high. Z = S + voice, a bee's buzz.
  1. S: corners of the lips relaxed or pulled back, teeth lightly together, tongue tip behind the bottom teeth (or pointing up). A thin, sharp hiss.
  2. SH: corners come in and the lips flare; the tongue tip lifts toward the middle of the mouth and the front arches close to the roof.
  3. Z is S with voice. Put a hand on your throat: zzz buzzes, sss doesn't. The letter s often says Z: is, easy, cousin, busy, music. 목에 손을 대고 zzz — 떨림이 있어야 Z

Korean habit

Before ㅣ, Korean ㅅ turns into “sh” (시 = shi): see → she, seat → sheet, and “sit” turns into a rude word. Z becomes ㅈ: zoo → 주, zero → 제로, busy → 비지.

Part 5 · VowelsRule 18 of 22Korean trap ★★★

EE vs. IH: sheep is tight, ship is relaxed

한국어 ㅣ 하나로 두 모음: EE는 혀를 높게·길게, IH는 힘 빼고 짧게

EE /i/ (sheep): tongue high and close to the roof, lips a little wide, longer. IH /ɪ/ (ship): more space above the tongue, jaw slightly open, relaxed, shorter.
  1. EE: tongue tip down, front of the tongue arched very close to the roof, corners of the lips pulled out: she, please, meet, police.
  2. IH: same tip, but more space between tongue and roof; the jaw may drop a little; everything relaxed: sit, fix, which, begin, busy.
  3. Rachel: “the tendency is to replace it with the EE vowel.” Most short-i spellings are IH: it, is, this, his, live, will, women, minute. 짧은 i 철자는 대부분 IH: it, is, this, live

Korean habit

ㅣ covers both, so ship = sheep, live = leave, fill = feel. It matters: said with IH, “beach” and “sheet” become swear words. Rachel made a whole video about those two pairs.

Part 5 · VowelsRule 19 of 22Korean trap ★★★

A jaw ladder: six → sex → sax

턱을 점점 더 내린다: IH(six) → EH(bed) → AE(bad) · ㅔ와 ㅐ를 다시 나누기

IH six, bit → EH sex, bed, men → AE sax, bad, man. Each step opens the jaw more.
  1. EH (bed): the jaw drops more than for IH; tongue tip forward and down; the middle of the tongue lifts and stays wide.
  2. AE (bad): the jaw drops a lot, the back of the tongue stretches up, the corners of the lips pull up and back. A bright, wide sound.
  3. Before M or N, AE relaxes into a short “uh”: man = mæ-ən, can, hand, exam. Before NG it moves toward AY: thanks, bank, gang. man은 “매-언”처럼 끝이 풀린다 · thank는 “쎄잉크” 쪽으로

Korean habit

In modern Seoul Korean ㅔ and ㅐ sound the same, so bed = bad, men = man, pen = pan. And “six” said too open sounds like “sex”, a pair Rachel says you'll want to master.

Part 5 · VowelsRule 20 of 22Korean trap ★★

Cut, cot, caught: the open vowels

UH ≈ 짧은 ㅓ · AH = 턱을 크게 연 ㅏ (o 철자여도!) · AW = AH + 둥근 입술

UH /ʌ/ cut: short, relaxed, close to ㅓ. AH /ɑ/ cot: jaw wide open like a big ㅏ, even when it's spelled o. AW /ɔ/ caught: AH with the lips flared (many Americans merge it with AH).
  1. UH: love, money, come, but, son, blood. The face stays relaxed, medium jaw drop, the back of the tongue presses down a little.
  2. AH: hot, job, stop, body, father, honest, October. A lot of jaw drop, the back of the tongue pressed down, so you see a dark space in the mouth.
  3. AW: law, talk, daughter, boss, long. Before R it rounds more: or, more, four, sore, core. AW + R: or, more, four — 입술을 더 둥글게

Korean habit

Reading the letter o as ㅗ: model → 모델 (American: MAH-dl), bottle → 보틀 (BAH-dl), job → 좁 (jahb), John → 존 (jahn). And cut vs cot needs ㅓ vs ㅏ kept apart.

Part 5 · VowelsRule 21 of 22Korean trap ★★★

ER is one sound: the vowel is the R

ER은 모음과 R이 한 소리: work ≠ 워크, girl ≠ 걸

bird = b + R + d. work = w + R + k. No ㅓ or ㅝ before the R, and almost no jaw drop.
  1. The R position, used as a vowel: lips push forward (corners in), the middle of the tongue lifts, the tip is pulled back touching nothing, very little jaw drop.
  2. Many spellings, one sound: her, bird, turn, work, learn, early, first, worth, Thursday.
  3. Unstressed -er is the same sound, just quicker: teacher, water, doctor (doc-tr), research. -er 끝소리도 같은 R 모음, 더 짧게

Korean habit

Adding a vowel or dropping the R: work → 워크 (= walk), girl → 걸, world → 월드, first → 퍼스트. Rachel on “work” in the Friends scene: “don't try to make a vowel… no jaw drop.”

Part 5 · VowelsRule 22 of 22Korean trap ★★

Diphthongs glide: go is “go-w”, say is “sa-y”

이중모음은 미끄러진다: go = 고우, say = 세이, home = 호움

A diphthong starts on one vowel and moves to another: aɪ my · aʊ now · ɔɪ boy · eɪ say · oʊ go (+ ju you).
  1. OH /oʊ/: go, no, show, home, phone, coat. The lips round more toward the end, toward a W.
  2. AY /eɪ/: say, made, late, cake, gauge. It ends toward EE.
  3. Letters don't tell you. The same letters can be different sounds (height vs. weight), which is why Rachel teaches with IPA. Check the dictionary's IPA for new words. 철자로는 알 수 없다 → 새 단어는 사전의 발음기호(IPA)로 확인

Korean habit

Korean ㅗ and ㅔ are steady, pure vowels: go → 고, no → 노, home → 홈, coat → 코트, say → 세. Without the glide, coat can sound like caught and “no” sounds clipped. Try 고우, 노우, 호움.
